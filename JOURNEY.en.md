# Learning Journey

These are a few things I’ve learned about how to study, how to work with AI, and how to decide whether a result tells me what I think it does.

## What I kept from embedded systems

When I started learning embedded systems, I tried to write down everything. I eventually learned to ask which things I needed to be able to do on my own, which concepts I needed to understand well, and which details I could look up later.

I’ve forgotten many of the ESP32 functions I once used. What stayed with me was the habit of checking my assumptions against what the hardware was actually doing. With my Arduino car, I could see that Bluetooth control worked and that the car still hit obstacles. Those two observations taught me more than a working demo alone would have.

## What it means to understand a model

When I was learning deep learning, I could explain attention and feed-forward layers separately, but I struggled to explain how a Transformer block worked as a whole. Following what happens to a token through the block helped me connect the pieces. Now I try to be more selective about what I study and give the ideas I keep enough time to understand how they fit together.

I also changed how I practice coding. I used to wonder whether I should be able to reproduce an implementation from memory. Now I pay more attention to whether I can read unfamiliar code, change it for a new purpose and work out why it fails. When I use AI to help with the code, those are still the things I need to be able to do.

## Working with AI in research

I often ask AI to write code, run programs and talk through an idea. Sometimes it spots something I missed and I change my mind. Sometimes it produces code that runs straight away. That’s when I try to pause and ask myself: suppose it gives me the result I expected. Would that result actually answer my question?

The abilities I most want to build are judgment, decision-making and the habit of checking evidence myself. Before trying an idea, I want to be able to explain why the question matters and what would make the comparison fair. Afterwards, I want to go back to the original records and see what the experiment tested and which explanations still fit.

I’m also learning when to stop. After spending time on a question, it takes some courage to admit that it’s too broad or that the evidence can’t distinguish between several explanations. I want to make that decision in time and carry what I learned into the next project.

If I put my name on a paper, I’m responsible for the decisions I made and every number I report. If someone asks where a number came from, I should be able to find the original record, explain how I checked it and say what conclusion it supports. That responsibility is mine even when AI helped write the code.

My research code, experiment records and related materials are kept in my private Research workspace.
